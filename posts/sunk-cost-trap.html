The Sunk Cost Trap: Why We Can't Let Go (And How to Fix It)

February 2026 · 7 min read

You've been sitting through a terrible movie for 90 minutes. You want to leave, but you think: "I already paid $15 for this ticket." So you stay. Congratulations - you've just fallen into the sunk cost trap, and it's costing you far more than the price of a movie ticket.

What Is the Sunk Cost Fallacy?

A sunk cost is any past investment of time, money, or effort that cannot be recovered. The sunk cost fallacy occurs when we let those unrecoverable investments influence our current decisions, even when continuing no longer serves our interests.

The rational approach is simple: only future costs and benefits should affect current decisions. What you've already spent is gone regardless of what you decide next. But our brains don't work that way.

Why We Fall for It

Several psychological forces drive the sunk cost fallacy:

Loss aversion. Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky showed that losses feel roughly twice as painful as equivalent gains feel good. Abandoning a project means admitting the loss, which our brains resist fiercely.

Consistency bias. We want to be consistent with our past decisions. Quitting feels like admitting we were wrong to start, which threatens our self-image as competent decision-makers.

The endowment effect. We overvalue what we already have or have invested in. The project we've spent months on feels more valuable simply because it's "ours."

Waste aversion. We're taught from childhood not to waste things. This useful heuristic becomes destructive when it prevents us from cutting losses on a failing path.

The Real-World Damage

The sunk cost fallacy doesn't just waste movie tickets. It keeps people in failing businesses, dead-end careers, and toxic relationships. It caused the Concorde jet to continue development long after it was clear the project would never be profitable (economists call it the "Concorde fallacy").

In investing, it's the force that makes people hold losing stocks far too long, thinking "I can't sell now - I'd be locking in a loss." Warren Buffett addresses this directly: "The most important thing to do if you find yourself in a hole is to stop digging."

Three Techniques to Break Free

1. The Clean Slate Test

Ask yourself: "If I were starting fresh today, with no prior investment, would I choose this same path?" If the answer is no, your sunk costs are biasing you. This simple reframe strips away the emotional weight of past investment and reveals the true forward-looking decision.

2. The Opportunity Cost Lens

Every hour, dollar, and unit of energy you spend continuing a failing endeavor has an opportunity cost. Instead of asking "What have I already invested?", ask "What else could I do with the time and resources I'd spend continuing?" Often, the alternative uses are far more valuable.

3. Pre-Commit to Exit Criteria

Before starting any significant project or investment, define the conditions under which you would walk away. Write them down. When those conditions are met, follow through. Having predetermined exit criteria removes the emotional decision-making that sunk costs exploit.

The Courage to Quit

Knowing when to quit is not weakness - it's wisdom. The best investors, entrepreneurs, and decision-makers share a common trait: they're willing to cut losses quickly and redirect resources to better opportunities. They understand that the goal isn't to justify past decisions, but to make the best decision right now, given what they know today.

As Charlie Munger says: "The best thing a human being can do is to help another human being know more." And knowing about the sunk cost trap is one of the most valuable pieces of self-knowledge you can gain.
